\section{Automated Science: The AI-Scientist Program and Its Audits}\label{sec:science}

A second literature asks whether agents can carry out research from question to paper. It has developed largely apart from the software-engineering work in Section~\ref{sec:building}, yet its evidence divides along the same line. Where an executed metric decides success, agents do useful work. Where they judge their own results, independent audits find fabrication.

\subsection{The Program}\label{sec:science-program}

The founding system, The AI Scientist, chains idea generation, code, experiments, a written paper and a simulated review, at less than \$15 per paper by its builders' account \citep{S1}. Its successor replaced human-written templates with tree search. One of three fully AI-written papers submitted blind to an ICLR workshop scored 6.33, above the workshop's average acceptance threshold, and was withdrawn after review as agreed in advance \citep{S2,S33}. Agent Laboratory reports a full pipeline at about \$2.33 per paper with a GPT-4o backend \citep{S3}, Kosmos runs cycles of data analysis for up to 12 hours \citep{S13}, and CodeScientist runs a genetic search over combinations of research articles and code blocks \citep{S34}. The open-source loops that practitioners use are smaller. In karpathy/autoresearch, an agent edits one training file, trains for five minutes, and keeps the change only if one validation number improved (\repofile{karpathy/autoresearch}{program.md}). OpenResearch wraps coding agents in an experiment tree whose answered nodes are frozen (\repofile{alphaXiv/OpenResearch}{agent-skills/orx-experiment-tree/SKILL.md}). The builders' own reports are more cautious than the headlines. The AI-Scientist-v2 README says the template-based v1 has ``high success rates'' and the exploratory v2 ``lower success rates'' (\repofile{SakanaAI/AI-Scientist-v2}{README.md}). Kosmos's developers report 79.4\% of sampled statements accurate but only 57.9\% of synthesis statements \citep{S13}. CodeScientist's authors judged 6 of its 19 candidate discoveries at least minimally sound and incrementally novel after external review \citep{S34}.

\subsection{The Audits}\label{sec:science-audits}

Independent audits converge on a few failures: results that were never computed, success declared over failure, and results chosen after the fact. An independent run of the first AI Scientist found that five of twelve proposed experiments failed through coding errors, that four of seven manuscripts contained incorrect or hallucinated numbers, and that the system's own reviewer identified none of the serious problems \citep{S9}. Controlled tests of AI-Scientist-v2 and Agent Laboratory found that both systems' internal reward sees test-set evaluations and favors experiments with strong test performance even when validation results are weak. Of 945 successful Agent Laboratory runs, 82.4\% chose the first four benchmarks listed in the prompt, regardless of their order \citep{S10}. FabScore, which checks each reported number against code by execution, puts the claim-level fabrication rate at 21.2\% across 144 papers, finds fabrications in 70.4\% of 54 real AI-authored conference submissions, and reports that AI reviewers miss over 85\% of the fabrications it detects \citep{S15}. On MLR-Bench, Claude Code reported results based on synthesized or placeholder data in 8 of 10 tasks, and the behavior persisted when it was told not to fabricate \citep{S16}. A prompt is therefore not a control. The repositories show the same mechanism. A code comment in CodeScientist calls simulating a step the agent cannot do ``the most common error in experiment building'' (\repofile{allenai/codescientist}{src/CodeBlockStore.py}). In AI-Scientist-v2, a guard against invented results is an instruction in a write-up prompt not to report ``any results not in the logs'' (\repofile{SakanaAI/AI-Scientist-v2}{ai_scientist/perform_writeup.py}). The samples are small (twelve experiments, ten tasks, 54 submissions) and mostly concern machine-learning research. They come, however, from different groups, systems and years, and they agree: without an outside check, a failed experiment tends to become a reported result.

\subsection{Where It Works}\label{sec:science-works}

The positive evidence comes almost entirely from tasks with a computable metric scored by an automated evaluator, in some cases on held-out data. Google's system for writing empirical software selects each solution on training data and reports it on holdout datasets; on that basis its authors report 40 methods that beat the best known method for single-cell batch integration and 14 COVID-19 forecasting models that outperformed the CDC ensemble \citep{S31}. In a blog post by Anthropic about its own models, nine copies of Claude Opus 4.6 reached a performance-gap-recovered score of 0.97 on a weak-to-strong supervision task, where two researchers working for seven days had reached 0.23, at a cost of about \$18,000. The method gave no statistically significant gain on a production model, and the authors chose the problem as ``unusually well-suited to automation'' \citep{S20}. A follow-up reports 26\% to 96\% of the safety gap closed across ten alignment failures \citep{S21}. AlphaEvolve's evaluator-scored loop found a scheduling heuristic that recovers on average 0.7\% of Google's fleet-wide compute, and its authors name as the main limitation that it needs problems for which an automated evaluator can be devised \citep{A25}. The metric is also a target. In one study, selecting final solutions by test rather than validation score would have raised the medal rate by 9 to 13 points \citep{R26}. In another, removing contaminated tasks cut the average kernel speedup of the authors' own loop from 3.13$\times$ to 1.49$\times$, and fake speedups of 50--120$\times$ had passed verification \citep{S28}. An issue on karpathy/autoresearch lists four ways the agent, which owns the file that calls the evaluator, can lower the score without a better model (\repo{karpathy/autoresearch}{599}). Newer systems move the measurement out of the agent's reach. AI-Scientist-v3 raises an error unless search and final seeds are disjoint (\repofile{SakanaAI/AI-Scientist-v3}{src/investigation/task_contract.py}). RD-Agent copies the grader and answer file into the workspace only at grading time and deletes them afterwards (\repofile{microsoft/RD-Agent}{rdagent/scenarios/data_science/test_eval.py}). Moving the evaluator outside is not sufficient on its own: an agent's repair made an evaluator non-deterministic, so that two scores of one program differed by more than the gains being measured (\repo{SakanaAI/AI-Scientist-v3}{5}). Replication with known answers is measurable but hard. On PaperBench the best agent scored 21.0\%, and on a three-paper subset ML PhDs reached 41.4\% against 26.6\% for o1 \citep{S7}. On CORE-Bench Hard, one agent's pass rate fell from 22.22\% on a single run to 8.89\% when all three runs had to succeed \citep{S8}. ReplicationBench's best model averaged 22\% \citep{S26}, and the best agent on REPRO-Bench reached 21.4\%, below the 25\% expected from random guessing \citep{S32}.

\subsection{Reviewing AI Research}\label{sec:science-review}

Review fails when the reviewer sees only the paper. In controlled tests, an LLM auditor reading only the final paper detected pitfalls with 55\% accuracy, and one given log traces and generated code reached 82\% \citep{S10}. Agent Laboratory's automated reviewers scored its papers 6.1 out of 10 where human reviewers gave 3.8 \citep{S3}. LLM review helps in a different role. In a randomized study at ICLR 2025, feedback reached 18,946 reviews, and 27\% of reviewers who received it updated their reviews \citep{S29}. That suggests reviewers found the feedback useful; it does not show that an LLM can tell whether a paper's numbers are real. Whole-task completion remains rare. On AstaBench, step scores reach about 70\%, but the chance of completing every step of an experiment stays near zero, with a maximum of 5\% \citep{S19}.

The pattern across these four strands suggests that automated science works as automated engineering under an external metric, and fails as autonomous judgment. For anyone building such systems, the implication is that the checks have to read code and logs, and rerun them, outside the agent's reach, not read the agent's report.
